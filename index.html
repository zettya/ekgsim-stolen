Reading a strip, in order

  1. Rate — roughly, count R waves in a 6-second strip ×10, or 300÷(large boxes between R waves).
  2. Regularity — are R-R intervals constant, occasionally irregular, or irregularly irregular?
  3. P waves — present? One before every QRS? Same shape each time (sinus origin)?
  4. PR interval — constant and 0.12–0.20s, or lengthening/dropping/absent?
  5. QRS width — narrow (<0.12s, above the AV node) or wide (≥0.12s, ventricular or aberrant)?

Working the same five questions every time is what makes rhythm ID fast — not memorizing shapes.

Cardiac arrest pathway

High-quality CPR is the constant: push hard and fast (≈100–120/min), full recoil, minimize interruptions, rotate compressors ~every 2 minutes to avoid fatigue. Everything else branches off the rhythm check.

Shockable — VF / pulseless VT

  • Defibrillate (unsynchronized) as soon as identified
  • Resume CPR immediately after the shock — don't pulse-check first
  • Epinephrine after the 2nd shock, then every 3–5 min
  • Consider amiodarone or lidocaine if refractory
  • Recheck rhythm every ~2 min

Non-shockable — asystole / PEA

  • No shock — resume/continue CPR
  • Epinephrine as soon as feasible, then every 3–5 min
  • Hunt the H's and T's (below) — PEA in particular is a "find the cause" rhythm
  • Recheck rhythm every ~2 min

Tachycardia with a pulse

First question: is the patient unstable from the rhythm — altered mental status, ischemic chest pain, hypotension, or signs of shock? If yes, that drives synchronized cardioversion regardless of QRS width. If stable, width and regularity decide the next step.

  • Unstable → synchronized cardioversion for organized tachyarrhythmias; sustained polymorphic VT is an exception and requires an immediate unsynchronized shock because reliable synchronization is not possible
  • Stable, narrow, regular → vagal maneuvers, then adenosine
  • Stable, narrow, irregular (e.g. AFib/flutter) → rate/rhythm strategy based on the patient; do not give AV-nodal blockers to a patient whose ventricular response is already slow
  • Stable, wide, regular → antiarrhythmic (e.g. amiodarone) with expert consult
  • Stable, wide, irregular → consider polymorphic VT or pre-excited AFib; expert consultation is important, and AV-nodal blockers should be avoided when pre-excited AF is suspected

Bradycardia with a pulse

Treat the patient, not the number — a rate under 60 that's perfusing well may need nothing but monitoring.

  • Symptomatic/unstable (hypotension, altered mentation, ischemia, shock) → atropine first line
  • If atropine fails or the block is likely infranodal (e.g. Mobitz II, complete block) → transcutaneous pacing, consider epinephrine or dopamine infusion
  • Asymptomatic/stable → monitor and observe; identify reversible causes

Electrical therapy — which one, and why it matters

Defibrillation (unsynchronized)

For pulseless, chaotic rhythms with no organized QRS to time against — VF and pulseless VT. Delivered as soon as the paddles/pads are charged and everyone is clear.

Synchronized cardioversion

For an organized tachyarrhythmia that still has a pulse. The unit times the discharge to the R wave so it can't land on the vulnerable T wave.

Transcutaneous pacing

For symptomatic bradycardia unresponsive to atropine, especially high-grade or infranodal block — paces the heart rather than shocking it.

Why sync mode exists: an unsynchronized shock delivered during the T wave (relative refractory period) can trigger VF — the "R-on-T" phenomenon. That's fine in pulseless arrest (there's no organized T wave to land on and nothing to lose), but a real hazard on any rhythm with a pulse. Sync mode is how a defibrillator avoids that when cardioverting.

High-yield edge cases

AF with a slow ventricular response

AF does not automatically mean “rate control.” If the ventricular rate is already slow, AV-nodal blockade can worsen bradycardia. Look for β-blockers, non-DHP calcium-channel blockers, digoxin, conduction disease, ischemia, hypoxia, electrolyte problems, or other reversible causes.

Polymorphic VT / torsades

Sustained polymorphic VT cannot be synchronized reliably. The immediate electrical treatment is an unsynchronized shock. If torsades is associated with prolonged QT, magnesium and correction of the cause help prevent recurrence; pause-dependent torsades may require expert-directed overdrive pacing.

Pre-excited AF

Irregular wide-complex tachycardia can represent AF conducting over an accessory pathway. Avoid AV-nodal blockers when pre-excitation is suspected; expert-directed therapy or synchronized cardioversion may be required depending on stability.

Bradycardia is a clinical diagnosis

A rate around 50 bpm is not automatically an emergency. Treat when the slow rate is causing symptoms or poor perfusion. In high-grade AV block, pacing should be readily available and may be needed early.

H's and T's — reversible causes to hunt during arrest

H's

  • Hypovolemia — control hemorrhage and give appropriate IV/IO fluid or blood resuscitation.
  • Hypoxia — optimize airway/ventilation and oxygenation; confirm effective ventilation.
  • Hydrogen ion (acidosis) — optimize CPR/ventilation and treat the underlying cause; bicarbonate is not routine, except in selected causes.
  • Hypo-/hyperkalemia — obtain/consider electrolytes and rapidly correct the specific potassium disorder; use cause-specific therapy.
  • Hypothermia — prevent further heat loss and provide active rewarming according to severity/protocol.

T's

  • Tension pneumothorax — immediate chest decompression, followed by definitive tube thoracostomy when appropriate.
  • Tamponade — urgent pericardial decompression; ultrasound can support recognition when available.
  • Toxins — identify the agent, provide supportive care and a specific antidote when indicated; involve toxicology/poison resources.
  • Pulmonary thrombosis — consider PE-directed therapy such as systemic thrombolysis or embolectomy/thrombectomy in selected arrest scenarios per protocol.
  • Coronary thrombosis — treat acute coronary occlusion; after ROSC, urgent coronary evaluation/revascularization is considered when indicated.

Important: H's & T's are not a checklist to complete only after several CPR cycles. Search for the most plausible reversible cause while CPR continues and treat it as soon as it is identified.
